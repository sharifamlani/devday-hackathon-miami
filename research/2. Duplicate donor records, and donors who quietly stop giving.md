# Project 2: Duplicate donor records, and donors who quietly stop giving

#### Research: closest current solutions, user journey, and JTBD

**Evidence boundary.** Tidepath Family Alliance and Denise are fictional; the challenge brief says the underlying problems came from a survey of 14 South Florida nonprofits. Treat the five hours/month, three spellings, missed December gift, and delayed discovery of an expired card as scenario details supplied for this challenge, not independently audited Tidepath measurements. The external cases below show similar patterns at other organizations; several are vendor-published case studies and should be read as customer-reported examples, not independent product evaluations.

##### Closest current solutions

| Solution | Closest match to this challenge | Evidence and remaining gap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bloomerang CRM** | Donor profiles/timelines, segmentation, and lapsed-donor outreach. | Eye Thrive says its donor history helped staff find donors who had “fallen through the cracks”; a personalized letter and CEO note reactivated one lapsed donor, who gave $2,500 ([customer story](https://bloomerang.com/results/eye-thrive)). This is a mature CRM workflow; the brief's opportunity is to make cross-source cleanup and next actions simpler for a part-time coordinator. |
| **Little Green Light (LGL)** | Lower-cost nonprofit donor database, giving history, reports, and lapsed status. | LGL marks a donor lapsed after 18 months by default and supports configurable giving-status reports ([knowledge base](https://help.littlegreenlight.com/article/73-constituent-giving-summary)). Tech Impact's comparison notes recurring-gift card expirations require a report and donor follow-up, and cultivation tasks are manually managed ([consumer guide](https://guides.techimpact.org/donor-management-systems/system/little-green-light/)). That is close to Denise's late detection problem. |
| **Salesforce Nonprofit CRM + Plauti** | Duplicate detection, bulk merge, and input-time validation for organizations already using Salesforce. | Diabetes Fonds reports that Salesforce's built-in duplicate review created manual backlogs and could not bulk-merge its cases; adding Plauti made checks part of entry, with staff tuning thresholds before automation ([case study](https://www.plauti.com/case-studies/how-data-quality-makes-life-easier-for-non-profits/)). Strong data-quality fit, but Salesforce and an add-on may be more system/admin overhead than a part-time coordinator needs. |
| **Bonterra EveryAction + Que** | Ask questions of donor records, create lapsed-donor segments, and plan follow-up. | The Women's Fund describes previously spending hours assembling year-by-year lapsed-donor lists; with Que, a fundraiser asks for donors who stopped giving, reviews the list, picks priorities, and plans tailored approaches ([customer story](https://www.bonterratech.com/case-studies/thirty-three-years-of-donor-history-finally-within-reach)). This is a close AI-assisted analog, though the case describes an established CRM and data store. |
| **Virtuous CRM+** | Connect fundraising data, clean up records, generate alerts, and cue personal stewardship. | Food Share of Ventura County reported four source systems, manual report exports, and month-end close taking three weeks; after implementation, close took three days, and gifts over $5,000 generated a personal follow-up task ([customer story](https://virtuous.org/case-studies/food-share-of-ventura-county/)). It demonstrates an integrated end-state, but implementation of a full CRM is outside a hackathon-sized prototype. |
| **DonorPerfect + Qgiv integration** | Import online donation/event data into a donor CRM without re-keying gifts. | Spay & Neuter Kansas City reported spending 5–6 hours weekly entering Qgiv transactions into DonorPerfect; their integration eliminated that manual entry ([customer story](https://www.qgiv.com/blog/how-qgiv-donorperfect-saved-spay-neuter-kansas-city-tons-of-time/)). This solves one source-to-CRM transfer, not necessarily identity resolution and proactive lapsed-gift triage across all channels. |

The competitive landscape is therefore not “no software exists.” It is a combination of donor CRMs, donation-platform integrations, reporting/segmentation, and sometimes a data-quality add-on. For Tidepath's scenario, the plausible wedge is a **lightweight exception-management layer** that accepts exports from several sources, identifies likely same-person records, explains why a regular gift looks late, and turns approved cases into a short, prioritized follow-up queue. It should complement existing databases rather than claim to replace a full CRM.

##### User journey and friction points

| Stage | Denise's current journey (from the brief) | Pain / risk | Useful product moment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Gifts arrive | Donations come through online giving, event tickets, gala auction, and mailed checks. | Each channel has its own list; formats, names, emails, and timing vary. | Import or connect source exports; preserve source, gift date, amount, and transaction reference. |
| 2. Monthly reconciliation | Download lists, import gifts into donor database, and type checks; about five hours/month. | Repetitive hand work consumes scarce fundraising time and invites omissions or duplicate gifts. | A staged import preview with row counts, validation, and a clear “already processed” check. |
| 3. Resolve identity | Denise sees “Rosa Delgado,” “Rosa M. Delgado,” and “R. Delgado,” with two emails. | Exact-match rules miss variants; fuzzy matching can incorrectly combine relatives or separate a shared household. | Rank possible matches with evidence and confidence; let Denise confirm, reject, or defer. Keep an audit trail and reversible merge. |
| 4. Communicate and report | The year-end letter goes out; totals are checked. | A donor can receive three copies; totals become unreliable and trust can be damaged. | Produce a deduplicated mailing/review list and reconcile gifts without deleting source history. |
| 5. Notice a missed gift | A regular December donor gives late or not at all; no alert brings it to Denise's attention. | A generic “lapsed after 18 months” label can be too late for an annual donor whose expected gift is due now. | Detect a missed expected giving window based on that donor's own cadence; show why it was flagged and let staff snooze/resolve it. |
| 6. Recover a recurring gift | A monthly donor's card expires; gifts stop and staff discover it months later. | There may be no timely CRM signal if the payment processor owns the subscription and status. | Import failed/expiring-payment events when available; otherwise label status “unknown” and prompt staff to verify—never infer a card failure from gift absence alone. |
| 7. Reach out and learn | Coordinator calls or writes, then should record outcome and next step. | Staff may lack time or context to make contact; without logging, the same donor can be contacted repeatedly or missed again. | Show a concise history and an editable, human-approved call/email draft; capture contact outcome, reply, and follow-up date. |

##### Jobs to Be Done lens (Christensen Institute)

The Christensen Institute describes Jobs Theory as understanding the progress people seek in context, including functional, emotional, and social dimensions ([overview](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/)). The following job statement and forces are our synthesis of Denise's challenge story, not an interview with Denise or an independently verified quotation.

**Core job statement:** “When gifts come in through several channels and I have limited fundraising hours, help me maintain one trustworthy donor history and spot a regular gift that is overdue early enough to make a personal, appropriate follow-up, so our supporters feel remembered and our fundraising can keep serving children and families.”

- **Functional job:** Reconcile incoming gift records, identify probable duplicates, preserve complete giving history, detect an unusual gap in a donor's normal cadence, and prioritize the next staff action.
- **Emotional job:** Feel confident that the totals and mailing list are trustworthy; feel that no committed supporter silently disappears because the coordinator was busy.
- **Social job:** Be seen by donors, colleagues, and the board as attentive and competent stewards of contributions; protect the organization's credibility.
- **Push away from the current way:** Separate channel exports, manual imports, name variants, duplicate year-end mail, inaccurate totals, and no exception alert.
- **Pull toward a better way:** One donor-level timeline, early and explainable alerts, fewer administrative hours, and a clear personal next step.
- **Anxieties / habit forces:** Fear of merging two different people, losing gift/source history, contacting someone who intentionally stopped, exposing donor information, or adding a tool that creates another database to maintain. The current spreadsheet/import routine persists because it is familiar and may be all a small team can support.
- **Desired outcomes:** Reduce manual import and review time; minimize false merges and duplicate acknowledgments; find missed expected gifts within days or a chosen grace window; ensure each flagged donor has an owner and disposition; preserve a source-linked audit trail.

##### What first-person evidence suggests

- A fundraiser at The Women's Fund said identifying lapsed donors previously required pulling reports and tracing gift history year by year; it took hours they did not have and often was not done. With an AI query workflow, she reviews likely lapsed donors and chooses a small priority list for tailored follow-up ([Bonterra customer story](https://www.bonterratech.com/case-studies/thirty-three-years-of-donor-history-finally-within-reach)). This closely corroborates “late gift / no timely call” as a prioritization problem.
- A database marketer at Diabetes Fonds described manual deduplication and contact checking as labor-intensive recurring work, with a backlog after campaigns because staff could not bulk merge records. Their case stresses tuning match thresholds and validating inputs before automating merges ([Plauti case study](https://www.plauti.com/case-studies/how-data-quality-makes-life-easier-for-non-profits/)). This supports review-first deduplication over silent auto-merge.
- Spay & Neuter Kansas City's CEO reported spending 5–6 hours each week on transaction entry between a donation platform and CRM before integration ([Qgiv case study](https://www.qgiv.com/blog/how-qgiv-donorperfect-saved-spay-neuter-kansas-city-tons-of-time/)). This makes Denise's five hours/month plausible as a smaller-volume scenario, while showing the same burden can be much larger elsewhere.
- Food Share's development chief described a “very hard” day across four systems and said month-end reporting took three weeks before integration; a fast personal thank-you after an alert reportedly led to an immediate second gift ([Virtuous customer story](https://virtuous.org/case-studies/food-share-of-ventura-county/)). This connects data access and timely action to donor experience, beyond data cleanliness alone.
- Eye Thrive's director of development said that reviewing older donor history helped recover supporters who had “fallen through the cracks”; one lapsed donor gave $2,500 after a personalized letter and CEO note ([Bloomerang customer story](https://bloomerang.com/results/eye-thrive)). The story illustrates a personal follow-up's potential, not a guaranteed impact of software.

##### Hackathon product implications

1. **Demo the whole loop:** import mixed-format synthetic gifts → propose matches → obtain human decisions → build one canonical donor timeline → flag a donor past their personal expected window → draft an editable, contextual outreach task → log the outcome.
2. **Use explainable cadence, not a blanket lapsed threshold:** for a donor who usually gives every December, flag in a configurable grace period after December; for monthly donors, surface a missed cycle quickly. The user must see the dates and rule behind each alert.
3. **Keep humans in control of identity and outreach:** never auto-merge ambiguous people; preserve all gift transactions and source rows; distinguish a duplicate *contact* from a duplicate *gift*; require approval before sending communications.
4. **Do not fabricate payment status:** “no gift recorded” is an observation, not proof of a failed/expired card. Only label payment failure when a source actually reports it; otherwise put it in a verification queue.
5. **Minimize sensitive data:** use fabricated names and synthetic gifts for the demo, consistent with the hackathon brief. Do not put payment-card details or real donor records into the prototype.
6. **Measure practical value:** time-to-reconcile, staff-confirmed match precision, unresolved/false-match rate, duplicate acknowledgments prevented, late regular donors surfaced within the chosen window, and percent of alerts resolved with an owner/action. Avoid claiming recovered revenue from a short demo.

**Research limits:** Product capabilities and testimonials are drawn from vendor or nonprofit-sector sources linked above, several of which are vendor-authored customer stories. They describe organizations with different sizes, data maturity, and budgets from fictional Tidepath. The research identifies adjacent products and observed workflows; it does not establish which tools Tidepath's surveyed nonprofits actually use or whether they would adopt a separate AI layer.
